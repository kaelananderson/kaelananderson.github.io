Understanding Community Resistance to Ebola Aid as a Product of Accumulated Grievance Reinforced by the America First Global Health Strategy

Kaelan Anderson · June 2026

Tuning in to global news stories has always felt like a good way to broaden my awareness of others’ experiences beyond my circles and my country. Consuming the news at any level, while educational in purpose, carries emotional burdens as well. I felt this weight listening to an episode of NPR's flagship daily news podcast, Up First, providing updates on the recent Ebola outbreak in DR Congo and Uganda. As the news of another fast and deadly spread of the disease settled with me, a burning question followed: Why do Ebola outbreaks continue to happen, and why does the disease always provide such a challenge to contain?

Many media outlets addressing this and similar questions critique the affected communities themselves. Their smoking gun points to the people of countries like DR Congo and Uganda, arguing that they are untrusting of the foreign aid. They explain it as a lack of understanding, mis-education, or false information, and therefore members of the community fail to adhere to crucial public health requirements. This, they argue, undermines response efforts to slow the spread and save infected lives, allowing the virus to move more rapidly.

While undeniably a factor in how populations respond to public health emergencies, the mis- or uneducation of a people fails to understand why distrust settles itself in these communities. It does not spark from misinformation; instead, it is deeply rooted in historical exploitation, extraction, and neglect by outsiders. Rather than dive into a mountain of historical examples, explaining how imperial impacts have resulted in discontent with global assistance, analysis of contemporary US global health policy proves more practical.

President Trump’s America First Global Health Strategy is unraveling into glaringly bold strategies to maintain the United States' international market dominance and further the extraction of African countries’ wealth. Framed as a plan offering new proposals in good faith, the reality for African countries in partnership with the US sets up an ever-uneven seesaw.

The illusion of ownership in this deal evaporates fast if we look at the poor intentions behind the US global health strategy. By decreasing or removing aid altogether, the US proposes that this allows African countries to begin building ownership of their health care infrastructure rather than relying on outside sources for their national health. Yet, how is a country able to build around itself when the products and medicines they need to care for its people are being largely produced by the power that formerly provided them as aid? The US is removing the help for a need from a system and replacing it with a privatized market. By withdrawing public aid while positioning American industry to fill the void for profit, the US policy change begins to etch itself in the defining features of neoliberal imperialism.

Profit motive takes more than one form here as well. Another effort is being made by the US to cut backdoor deals regarding access to new markets, such as mining minerals in Zambia. The vocabulary used to justify these exploitations has changed throughout the decades of one-sided relationships between Western powers and African nations. “Civilizing mission” became “partnership.” “Conquest” became “compact.” There has been a shift from colonial frameworks to neoliberal imperialism. Still, the pattern of arriving with demands dressed as generosity has not changed.

The most acutely bleak aspect of this calculated extraction of wealth is their relentless appetite to obtain data. Yet another negotiation point for them, gathering large amounts of health-related data from populations whose health data has historically been underrepresented in global datasets can lead to various profitable outcomes. Development and manufacturing of new medicines and vaccines, and the training of machine learning models on never-before-seen African health data, are viewed as an attractive prize for any country in this age of big data. However, due to recent experiences of data sharing with foreign powers, many African countries are hesitant to agree to these terms. During the height of COVID-19 cases throughout much of the continent, many African countries were the last in line to receive the first doses of vaccines developed with the help of their data sharing, while citizens in wealthier countries were receiving their booster shots. What emerges across each of these agreements is not a health strategy but a series of market transactions dressed as one.

Experience in healthcare has made one principle clear: the measure of any health strategy is whether it delivers care to the people who need it. The current administration disregards this entirely. “America First” is not a global health strategy. It does not prioritize, let alone provide, the necessary aid for those in need. And from a data perspective, it gives me fear for the future of the value we place on obtaining data while not providing any sense of good virtue in return. With our current attitudes, I would be reluctant to see any sort of call to action to help communities in need on a global stage in the face of the threat of a public health crisis, and I find it hard to foresee a future where our data models help anyone else besides our most profitable patriotic patrons.

The message being communicated to African countries is being heard loud and clear. Aid is conditional, and to receive it, one must capitulate. This is not a new message, yet it is one that has been delivered through centuries of exploitation and neglect. When Ebola responders arrive in DR Congo and Uganda, they arrive as representatives of the same systems that have extracted, conditioned, and withheld. From a community trust level, it is easier to see where these fragile sentiments come from when aid arrives for Ebola outbreaks. If help is only awarded through coercion, one may find it appropriate to reject such presence in their homes.
