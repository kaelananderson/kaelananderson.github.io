Preparing San Diego 311 Encampment Data for SaTScan

Tutorial · Python · 2024

This tutorial walks through the data preparation pipeline for running a space-time cluster analysis on publicly available 311 service request data from the City of San Diego. The data comes from the Homelessness Hub San Diego ArcGIS portal maintained by UC San Diego.

The goal is to take a raw CSV of encampment reports and produce the two input files that SaTScan requires: a case file (.cas) recording when and where reports occurred, and a geography file (.geo) mapping each location ID to its coordinates. Along the way, we will look at the data carefully enough to make defensible modeling decisions — and understand what we can and cannot conclude from the results.

Step 1 — Download and load the data

Download the dataset from the 311 Encampment Reports page. Click Download and choose the CSV option. I use the 2024 calendar year — a full year matters for building a clean temporal baseline. If you pull a multi-year file, everything below still applies, but pay attention to the study period at the end.

import pandas as pd import matplotlib.pyplot as plt CSV_PATH = "311_Encampment_Reports_2C_2024_2C_City_of_San_Diego.csv" df = pd.read_csv(CSV_PATH) print(df.shape) df.head()
59,059 rows, 26 columns

Each row is one 311 report — not one encampment, and not one person. That distinction matters more than almost anything else here, and we will come back to it. For cluster detection, only three things matter from those 26 columns:

What SaTScan needsColumn in this dataset
When it happeneddate_requested

How many at that place and timecomputed by counting

The other columns — council_district, status, case_age_days — are useful for exploration and for interpreting results afterward, but they are not model inputs. Before discarding them, we will use them.

Step 2 — Explore before you model

Nearly every mistake in spatial analysis is a mistake about what the data is. Those are much easier to catch with a plot than with a p-value. Let's look at what we have before touching any modeling code.

print(df["service_name"].value_counts()) print() print(df["status"].value_counts())

Every row is an Encampment report, so no subsetting is needed for this export. We will still write the filter explicitly below — if you re-download this dataset next year and the city has added a second service type, a silent change is worse than a loud one.

Now let's look at the temporal distribution.

dates = pd.to_datetime(df["date_requested"], utc=True).dt.date weekly = pd.Series(1, index=pd.to_datetime(dates)).resample("W").sum() fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 3.6)) ax.plot(weekly.index, weekly.values, color="#c0392b", lw=1.6) ax.set_title("311 Encampment Reports per Week, San Diego 2024", fontsize=13) ax.set_xlabel("week") ax.set_ylabel("reports") ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False) ax.grid(alpha=0.25) plt.tight_layout() plt.show()

Reports range from roughly 60 to 1,570 per week — a twenty-six-fold swing within a single year.

Does that mean the number of encampments swung twenty-six-fold? Almost certainly not. Report volume responds to enforcement pushes, media coverage, weather, and outreach campaigns. What we are measuring is reporting behavior, which is related to the underlying phenomenon but is not the same variable. SaTScan will find clusters in whatever you give it — if you feed it reports, it finds clusters of reporting.

Now let's look at the geographic distribution — where are reports coming from?

# comm_plan_name has inconsistent casing ('Downtown' vs 'DOWNTOWN'). # .str.title() folds them together before counting. cp = df["comm_plan_name"].str.title().value_counts().head(12).sort_values() fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7.5, 4.6)) ax.barh(cp.index, cp.values, color="#2c3e50") ax.set_title("Top Community Plan Areas by Encampment Reports (2024)", fontsize=13) ax.set_xlabel("reports") ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False) plt.tight_layout() plt.show()

Downtown leads with over 20,000 reports — roughly a third of the city's total. Uptown, Mid-City: City Heights, and North Park follow at a distance.

Here is where it gets interesting. Downtown will almost certainly show up as a cluster, but of course it will: it has the most reports, the densest foot traffic, and the most people around to call. A method that only tells us "Downtown has a lot of encampment reports" has told us nothing we couldn't get from this bar chart. The reason to use SaTScan is that it asks a sharper question: given the baseline distribution, was there a specific window of time when a specific area produced more reports than its own typical rate? That's a question about anomalies relative to expectation — and the bar chart can't answer it.

Step 3 — Build the analysis dataset

Subset to encampment reports only, keep the three columns we need, and parse the date.

camp_data = df[df["service_name"].str.strip().str.lower() == "encampment"].copy() camp_data = camp_data[["service_request_id", "date_requested", "lat", "lng"]] print(camp_data.shape)
# Normalize to day resolution. # Timestamps are UTC ('2024/01/01 08:32:00+00'); utc=True makes that explicit. camp_data["date"] = pd.to_datetime(camp_data["date_requested"], utc=True).dt.date camp_data["date"] = pd.to_datetime(camp_data["date"]) camp_data = camp_data.drop(columns=["date_requested"]) camp_data.head()

A subtle point: San Diego is 7–8 hours behind UTC. A report filed at 5 pm local on January 1st lands at 00:32 UTC on January 2nd, so a handful of reports get assigned to the next day. For day-resolution analysis over a full year this is negligible — but if you ever move to hourly analysis, convert to America/Los_Angeles first.

Step 4 — Clean coordinates

print("missing coordinates:", camp_data[["lat", "lng"]].isna().any(axis=1).sum()) camp_data = camp_data.dropna(subset=["lat", "lng"]) # Guard against (0, 0) 'null island' -- a geocoding failure mode that lands a phantom # cluster in the Gulf of Guinea rather than producing a visible NaN. camp_data = camp_data[(camp_data["lat"] != 0) & (camp_data["lng"] != 0)] print(camp_data.shape)

Only 17 records lack coordinates, leaving 59,042 — a 0.03% loss, about as painless as geographic data gets. The null-island check finds nothing here, but it's worth keeping. When a geocoder fails, it often returns (0, 0) rather than NaN, and (0, 0) is a valid-looking coordinate that passes every range check silently.

Step 5 — Decide what counts as "the same place"

SaTScan does not accept raw point data. It expects a set of locations — each with a stable ID and one coordinate pair — and a separate table recording how many cases occurred at each location on each date. That means we have to decide when two reports happened at "the same place."

The lever is coordinate precision. Decimal degrees of latitude translate to ground distance roughly like this:

Decimal placesApprox. resolution
6~10 cm
5~1 m
4~11 m
3~110 m

Let's see what each precision level does to the number of distinct locations.

for p in [6, 5, 4, 3]: n = ( camp_data.assign(a=camp_data["lat"].round(p), b=camp_data["lng"].round(p))[["a", "b"]] .drop_duplicates() .shape[0] ) print(f"{p} decimals -> {n:6,d} locations, {len(camp_data)/n:5.2f} reports per location")
6 decimals -> 56,470 locations, 1.05 reports per location 5 decimals -> 53,951 locations, 1.09 reports per location 4 decimals -> 31,095 locations, 1.90 reports per location 3 decimals -> 8,337 locations, 7.08 reports per location

At 6 decimals, almost every report is its own unique location. Two calls about the same tent on the same block, geocoded a few centimeters apart, become two unrelated places — we have discarded exactly the repetition that cluster detection exists to find.

At 3 decimals, 110 meters is more than a city block downtown, so we smear genuinely distinct spots together and manufacture density that isn't there.

Four decimal places (~11 m, roughly one address) is a reasonable middle ground: 31,095 locations averaging 1.9 reports each. I'll use 4, but it is a choice — not a discovery.

Don't take my word for it. Run the final analysis at both COORD_PRECISION = 4 and COORD_PRECISION = 3. Clusters that persist across both are findings worth reporting. Clusters that disappear when you nudge the precision are artifacts of the rounding — and the honest thing to do is say so.

COORD_PRECISION = 4 camp_data["latitude"] = camp_data["lat"].round(COORD_PRECISION) camp_data["longitude"] = camp_data["lng"].round(COORD_PRECISION) camp_data = camp_data.drop(columns=["lat", "lng"]) # Assign a stable integer ID to each distinct coordinate pair unique_coords = ( camp_data[["latitude", "longitude"]] .drop_duplicates() .sort_values(["latitude", "longitude"]) .reset_index(drop=True) ) unique_coords["location_id"] = range(1, len(unique_coords) + 1) camp_data = camp_data.merge(unique_coords, on=["latitude", "longitude"], how="left") print("unique locations:", len(unique_coords)) camp_data.head()
counts = camp_data.groupby("location_id").size().rename("n").reset_index() geo = unique_coords.merge(counts, on="location_id").sort_values("n") fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.4, 6.8)) sc = ax.scatter(geo["longitude"], geo["latitude"], c=geo["n"], s=4 + geo["n"] * 1.2, cmap="inferno_r", alpha=0.75, linewidths=0, vmin=1, vmax=20) ax.set_title("Encampment Reports by Location, 2024", fontsize=13) ax.set_xlabel("longitude") ax.set_ylabel("latitude") ax.set_aspect(1 / 0.84) cb = fig.colorbar(sc, ax=ax, shrink=0.6) cb.set_label("reports at location (capped at 20 for color scale)") ax.spines[["top", "right"]].set_visible(False) plt.tight_layout() plt.show() print("most reports at one location:", geo["n"].max())

The shape of the city emerges from the reports alone — no basemap loaded. The dense mass is Downtown; you can trace corridors along the I-8 and El Cajon Blvd. One location drew 211 reports in a single year — a report roughly every 1.7 days, all year, from one ~11 m square. That is either a genuinely persistent encampment or a very persistent caller, and it's worth knowing which before you build an argument around it.

Step 6 — Build the case file (.cas)

The case file records counts by (location, date). We aggregate all individual reports down to that grain.

aggregated_df = camp_data.groupby(["date", "location_id"]).size().reset_index(name="cases") aggregated_df = aggregated_df.sort_values(["date", "location_id"]) aggregated_df["case_id"] = range(1, len(aggregated_df) + 1) cas_df = aggregated_df[["case_id", "date", "cases", "location_id"]].copy() cas_df["date"] = cas_df["date"].dt.strftime("%Y/%m/%d") print(cas_df.shape) print("total cases:", cas_df["cases"].sum()) print("rows with more than one case:", (cas_df["cases"] > 1).sum()) cas_df.head()

55,784 rows carrying 59,042 cases. Only 2,636 rows have a count above 1, meaning same-day repeat reports at the same spot are the exception, not the rule.

There's a judgment call hiding in that groupby. If three neighbors each call about the same tent on the same afternoon, we record 3 cases, not 1. Whether that's correct depends entirely on your research question: three calls are three genuine signals of community concern, but they are also one tent. Decide deliberately, and write down which framing you chose.

import os OUT_DIR = "satscan_usable" os.makedirs(OUT_DIR, exist_ok=True) cas_path = os.path.join(OUT_DIR, "2024_camps.cas") cas_df.to_csv(cas_path, index=False, header=True) print("wrote", cas_path)

Step 7 — Build the geography file (.geo)

The geography file is the lookup table: one row per location ID with its coordinates. Every location referenced in the .cas file must appear here, or SaTScan will refuse to run.

geo_df = ( camp_data[["location_id", "latitude", "longitude"]] .drop_duplicates(subset=["location_id"]) .sort_values("location_id") ) # Sanity-check coordinate ranges before writing if not ((geo_df["latitude"].between(-90, 90)) & (geo_df["longitude"].between(-180, 180))).all(): raise ValueError("Invalid coordinates found.") geo_path = os.path.join(OUT_DIR, "2024_camps.geo") geo_df.to_csv(geo_path, index=False, header=True) print(geo_df.shape) geo_df.head()

Step 8 — Validate before handing off

SaTScan's error messages are terse and its silent failures are worse. It is much cheaper to assert our invariants here than to debug a suspicious cluster three hours from now. Four things must hold: coordinates map one-to-one with location IDs, no ID is duplicated, every location in the case file exists in the geography file, and the case counts survived aggregation intact.

# Coordinates map 1:1 with location IDs, no duplicates in either direction assert not geo_df.duplicated(subset=["latitude", "longitude"]).any(), "duplicate coordinate pairs" assert not geo_df.duplicated(subset=["location_id"]).any(), "duplicate location_ids" # Referential integrity: every location in .cas must exist in .geo assert set(cas_df["location_id"]).issubset(set(geo_df["location_id"])), \ "cas references unknown locations" # Conservation: no cases invented or lost during aggregation assert cas_df["cases"].sum() == len(camp_data), "case count does not match record count" check = camp_data.groupby(["date", "location_id"]).size().reset_index(name="expected") check["date"] = check["date"].dt.strftime("%Y/%m/%d") merged = check.merge(cas_df, on=["date", "location_id"], how="inner") assert len(merged) == len(cas_df) and (merged["expected"] == merged["cases"]).all(), \ "count mismatch between source and cas" print("All checks passed.") print(f" {len(geo_df):,} locations") print(f" {len(cas_df):,} case rows") print(f" {cas_df['cases'].sum():,} total cases")
from datetime import datetime min_date, max_date = cas_df["date"].min(), cas_df["date"].max() start = datetime.strptime(min_date, "%Y/%m/%d") end = datetime.strptime(max_date, "%Y/%m/%d") print(f"Study Period Start: {min_date}") print(f"Study Period End: {max_date}") print(f"Total days: {(end - start).days + 1}")

366 days — 2024 was a leap year. Write down the start and end dates; SaTScan asks for them explicitly and will happily run with wrong values.

Step 9 — Configure and run SaTScan

Download SaTScan free from satscan.org. Create a new session and point it at the two files we produced.

Input tab

  • Case File: satscan_usable/2024_camps.cas
  • Coordinates File: satscan_usable/2024_camps.geo
  • Coordinates: Latitude/Longitude
  • Time Precision: Day
  • Study Period: 2024/01/01 to 2024/12/31

Analysis tab

  • Type of Analysis: Retrospective Space-Time
  • Probability Model: Space-Time Permutation
  • Scan For Areas With: High Rates

The Space-Time Permutation model is the right choice here because we have no denominator — no population-at-risk data to convert raw counts into rates. Most SaTScan models require one. Space-Time Permutation sidesteps the issue by using the data as its own control: given how many reports this location produced all year, and given how many reports the whole city produced during this time window, is the count in this place during this window surprising? No external denominator required.

Under Output, enable the Google Earth / KML export to see clusters on a map. The default maximum spatial cluster size (50% of the population) is usually too permissive — try 10% under the Spatial Window tab. Under Temporal Window, 30 days is a reasonable starting point if you're hunting for acute flare-ups rather than season-long shifts.

Step 10 — Read the output honestly

SaTScan returns a ranked list of clusters — each with a center, radius, date range, observed-to-expected ratio, and p-value. Before treating the top result as a finding, three things deserve scrutiny.

The p-values come from Monte Carlo simulation. SaTScan shuffles your data hundreds of times and asks how often chance produces something this extreme. That's a sound procedure, but it tests one narrow null: no space-time interaction, given the observed margins. It does not test "nothing interesting is happening," and it cannot rescue you from a biased sample.

Clusters of reports are not clusters of encampments. A neighborhood that organizes a reporting drive will light up. A neighborhood where people have concluded that calling 311 accomplishes nothing will go dark — regardless of what's actually there. This gap is not random; it correlates with language, immigration status, housing tenure, and trust in city government.

Some clusters are administrative artifacts. If the city ran an enforcement sweep in March in one district, you'll find a March cluster there. It's real — but it's a cluster of city activity, not of the underlying phenomenon. Before interpreting any cluster, check the news and the city's outreach calendar for that window.

The right posture is the one you'd take with any exploratory analysis: results generate hypotheses, they don't confirm them. A cluster is an invitation to go find out what happened there, not a conclusion about what happened there.

This dataset is about unhoused people — some of the most surveilled and least powerful residents of the city. A map of "hot spots" can inform outreach and services, or it can direct enforcement at people whose actual problem is that they have nowhere to go. The math is identical either way. The choice of use is yours, and it is not a technical one.

Extensions and next steps

Once you have baseline results, here are worthwhile directions to push on:

  • Test sensitivity to precision. Re-run at COORD_PRECISION = 3 and 5. Clusters that survive all three are worth reporting; those that don't are worth flagging.
  • Reports vs. unique incidents. Collapse same-day duplicate reports at a location, re-run, and compare. If the top cluster depends on counting three calls about one tent as three cases, that's a finding about your pipeline.
  • Seasonal splits. Divide the year into quarters and run each separately. Do clusters move, or do the same blocks recur each quarter?
  • Use the status column. The status field separates Closed from Referred. Do clusters of referred cases differ geographically from clusters of all cases?
  • Add a denominator. If you can build a population-at-risk layer from Census tract data, the Poisson model becomes available — and asks a sharper question than permutation alone.
  • Go multi-year. Pull 2023 and 2024, run a Prospective Space-Time analysis, and identify which clusters are still active at the end of the study period rather than which ones ever existed.
